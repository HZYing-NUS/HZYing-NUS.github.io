# OpenAI Will Not Collapse This Year, but the Myth Is Dead

Over the past couple of days, I saw a very provocative claim: OpenAI will collapse this year. The reason this claim spreads so easily is that it is not completely baseless. Massive ongoing losses, the controversy around Sora being shut down, frequent leadership changes, talent leaving, a changing relationship with Microsoft, and Anthropic's fast rise in enterprise and developer scenarios: when you put all these signals together, they form a very complete story. OpenAI is in trouble. The first tier of AI is changing. Anthropic is about to take the throne.

It is a very smooth story. But I have become increasingly wary of stories that are too smooth. Often, they are not judgments. They are scripts. My own view is clear: OpenAI will not collapse this year. But another sentence matters just as much: OpenAI's mythological era is over. Many people confuse these two things. They turn the bankruptcy of a myth directly into the bankruptcy of a company. There is one crucial step missing in between: who has actually stopped paying?

---

## 1. Losses Are Not a Death Certificate. Customers Leaving Is.

When judging whether a startup will die, you cannot only look at whether it is losing money. This is especially true for a company like OpenAI. It is not an ordinary SaaS company, nor is it a small cash-flow business. It sits at the intersection of AI infrastructure, consumer entry points, enterprise workflows, and capital-market narratives. A company like that will burn money. At certain stages, burning money is itself part of how it fights for position.

The real question is not whether it is burning money, but whether people are still willing to pay for its future. That payment has at least three layers. The first is users paying: are ChatGPT's paid users still there, and when ordinary people say they use AI, is ChatGPT still the first thing that comes to mind? The second is enterprises paying: is OpenAI still on the default model list inside enterprise workflows, and is it still hard to avoid in procurement, customer service, coding, knowledge bases, and automation processes? The third is capital and compute paying: can it still raise money, can it still get compute, and are Microsoft, Oracle, Nvidia, and various investors still willing to stand on the same boat with it?

If all three layers break at the same time, then OpenAI is truly in danger. But many of the negative signals we are seeing now look more like symptoms of physical discomfort: losses, a heavier organization, leadership turnover, renegotiated partnerships, product-line contraction, and competitors catching up. These problems are serious, but they are not cardiac arrest.

What would cardiac arrest look like? Users stop using it. Developers migrate away. Enterprises do not renew. Financing dries up. Core talent no longer believes in the company. Only when it reaches that point do we have a death certificate. Right now, the evidence is not enough to say OpenAI will collapse this year.

---

## 2. The Most Dangerous Parts of That OpenAI Collapse Prediction Are Its Unverified Assumptions

I watched the video that said OpenAI would collapse this year. It is not worthless. In fact, it captured a real change: the AI industry is moving from who looks more dazzling to who can actually make money. Over the past two years, many people judged AI companies only by model capability, launch events, parameter counts, and demo effects. Whoever released an astonishing demo looked like they were standing at the doorway of the future.

But now the question has changed. Who can turn models into cash flow? Who can make enterprises keep paying? Who can bring compute costs down? Who can make product revenue cover infrastructure investment? These are the real questions the industry has to answer after entering its second phase.

The problem with that video is that it jumped from a correct direction to an overly aggressive conclusion: because OpenAI is burning money badly, OpenAI will collapse this year. That leap is too large. Several key assumptions inside it need question marks. For example, the claim that Anthropic is already profitable, or at least no longer losing money, is unverified. Revenue growth, gross-margin improvement, positive cash flow, and accounting profit are not the same thing.

Another example is the claim that OpenAI loses 120 yuan for every 100 yuan of revenue. When a precise number like that has no clear source, we should be even more careful. The claim that Microsoft's support cannot last longer than six months is also a judgment, not a fact. Why six months? Why not three months, or eighteen months? The explanation is missing. As for the claim that OpenAI has no backing, that is too crude. OpenAI is not an internal Google department, but it has the ChatGPT entry point, an API ecosystem, enterprise contracts, a Microsoft relationship, and a capital-market narrative. These are not nothing.

So I do not suggest treating that video as strong evidence that OpenAI will collapse within the year. It is more like a short narrative with a strong sense of direction. It is right about one thing: OpenAI is no longer an object of blind faith. But it turns the collapse of the myth into the collapse of the company, and that step does not yet stand.

---

## 3. Anthropic Getting Stronger Does Not Mean OpenAI Has Become Second-Tier

This is another judgment people often mix together. Anthropic has indeed been very strong over the past six months. Claude Code has become highly visible among developers. Enterprise trust, B2B sales, and API use cases are all moving forward. In public reports, Anthropic's valuation, revenue growth, and enterprise penetration are frequently compared with OpenAI's. If you only look at enterprise and developer scenarios, it is easy to reach one conclusion: OpenAI has been caught by Anthropic, and in some areas even overtaken.

I accept that judgment. What I do not accept is the next sentence: therefore OpenAI has become second-tier. These two companies are not fighting on exactly the same battlefield. Anthropic is more like a high-end toolbox. OpenAI is more like a default entry point. The strength of a toolbox is that it is professional, stable, trustworthy, and useful. The strength of a default entry point is mindshare, habit, distribution, and ecosystem inertia.

Ask an ordinary person today: do you use AI? They probably will not first talk about APIs, model routing, or enterprise workflows. They will say: I use ChatGPT. This is OpenAI's most valuable asset. It is not just a model company. For many ordinary people, it is the first doorway into AI.

Entry points are strange things. They are not always technically the strongest, but as long as user habits remain, they are hard to kill suddenly. A search engine does not always give you the perfect answer, but you still instinctively open that default entry point. Many apps on your phone can replace part of WeChat's functionality, but you would not therefore say WeChat is about to disappear. In e-commerce, there are many cheaper platforms, but once default mindshare forms, it is not something a few negative headlines can destroy.

OpenAI's problem now is that it no longer monopolizes the best AI experience. But it has not yet lost the default AI entry point. These are very different things.

---

## 4. What I Really Watch Is Not Valuation or Executive Gossip

If I want to judge whether OpenAI is falling behind, I will not only stare at launch events. I will not only stare at who left, who joined Anthropic, who is in conflict with Microsoft, or who raised another huge round. These pieces of information are useful, but they are not hard enough. What really matters are a few indicators that can verify whether habits are breaking.

The first is whether ChatGPT's monthly active users and paid users are declining significantly. If ordinary users are still using it, the consumer entry point is still there. The second is whether enterprise renewal rates are falling significantly, especially among companies that have already connected OpenAI to real workflows, not trials or proofs of concept. The third is whether API volume is being clearly squeezed by Anthropic or Google. Developers are practical. Whoever is useful, cheap, and stable will win migration. The fourth is whether Microsoft is clearly lowering OpenAI's priority inside its own products. A more transactional partnership is not the same as a breakup. The real danger would be Microsoft starting to systematically route around OpenAI. The fifth is whether OpenAI can continue to secure large financing or compute support. For AI companies now, the final competition is often less about launch events and more about capital-expenditure endurance.

If these five indicators worsen at the same time, that would be an earthquake. One bad piece of news is not enough. Executive departures are not enough. Anthropic surpassing it in valuation is not enough. Heavy losses are not enough. Renegotiating the Microsoft agreement is still not enough. The truly bad signal is habit breaking. Enterprises stop using it, developers stop using it, ordinary users stop using it, and capital stops believing in it. That is what OpenAI should really fear.

---

## 5. What You Should Really Be Alert To Is Not Whether OpenAI Will Collapse

To me, the most interesting part of this issue is not OpenAI itself. It is the psychology it reveals among many AI entrepreneurs: you think you are judging the industry, but you are actually looking for a sense of safety. Someone says OpenAI will collapse, and you get nervous. Someone says Anthropic will win, and you get excited. Someone says Google will counterattack, and you start wavering again. Someone says open-source models are the future, and you want to change direction again.

But think about it carefully. If OpenAI does not collapse, does that mean you will definitely succeed? If OpenAI collapses, does that mean you definitely have no opportunity? Neither is true. For super individuals and small teams, tying your fate to any one model company is itself dangerous.

OpenAI is a whale. Anthropic is also a whale. Google, Meta, and xAI are whales too. You are not a whale. You are someone raising goldfish in a small stream. What you should really care about is not whether one whale will run aground, but whether there is a specific user today who is willing to pay for the result you have packaged.

This question is small, but it is far more useful than whether OpenAI will collapse. Macro judgments are addictive. The first payment makes you sober. I increasingly believe this sentence: the opposite of anxiety is specificity. When you become specific enough to name one user, one scenario, and one payment action, many grand fears automatically disappear. You do not need to predict the entire ocean. You need to know whether the fish food in your hand is something any fish will actually eat.

---

## 6. My Conclusion: OpenAI Will Not Collapse, but It Will Become Ordinary

So, back to the original question: will OpenAI collapse this year? My judgment is that the probability is low. Unless several hard events occur, such as financing breaking down, a major legal or governance accident, a complete rupture with Microsoft, large-scale enterprise customer migration, a clear decline in ChatGPT users, or API usage being systematically replaced, the claim that OpenAI will collapse this year is too aggressive.

But will OpenAI continue to hold the mythological status it had in the past? I also think that will be difficult. It will go from being the sole protagonist of the AI era to becoming a very strong, very expensive, very heavy, and very troublesome giant-level AI company. Its valuation narrative will shrink. Organizational complexity will rise. Its relationship with Microsoft will become more transactional. Anthropic will continue to beat it in some enterprise and developer scenarios. Google, Meta, and open-source models will keep eating away at its boundaries.

This is not collapse. It is disenchantment. The biggest change for OpenAI is not falling from the first tier into the second tier. It is coming down from the altar and entering the real business world. On the altar, you only need people to believe in the future. In the business world, you must make customers keep paying. These are two completely different games.

---

## 7. A Judgment Framework That Is Actually Useful

The next time you see a claim that some AI company is about to collapse, or some model company is about to be deified, I suggest not rushing to choose a side. Ask five questions first: are its users still there? Are its customers still renewing? Are its developers still calling the API? Does capital still believe in it? Has its default entry point been replaced?

If these five things have not broken, do not casually say it is dying. If these five things start breaking at the same time, do not hold it up with sentiment. Industry judgment is most afraid of two kinds of people: those who are always bullish because they treat the future as faith, and those who are always bearish because they treat crisis as traffic. The useful judgment is not who speaks more fiercely, but who can break a mess into several verifiable indicators.

OpenAI will not collapse this year, but OpenAI no longer deserves blind faith. That is the judgment I think is closer to reality. What died was not the company. It was the myth.
