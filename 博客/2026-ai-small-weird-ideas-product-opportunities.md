# In the AI Era, the Most Valuable Things Are Not Big Ideas, but Weird Little Thoughts

A few days ago, I noticed something interesting: some people are raising digital cats on their computer desktops, some are building digital worship tools, some are turning novels into interactive games where readers can choose the plot, and some are making life autobiography tools that help you track your days, emotions, and daily fragments. In the past, my first reaction would probably have been: what is the point of this? But now it feels different. I have started taking these things seriously.

Because behind them is an important shift: **in the AI era, many small thoughts that used to look strange now have a chance to grow into products.** Not because these ideas have suddenly become grand, but because the cost of turning an idea into something real is falling quickly. In the past, if you had an idea and wanted to turn it into a product, you needed design, development, deployment, and operations. If any step got stuck, the idea died in your head. Now you can use AI to write code, design interfaces, generate copy, and break down requirements. This changes one thing: what used to require a team can now be tried by one person; what used to take months to validate can now become a clickable web page in a few days.

At that point, what becomes scarce is not just technical skill. It is whether you can see the tiny, specific, easily ignored needs. Recently, I used Jizhidao API to look through related articles in the WeChat public account ecosystem, and I found that many people are already running in directions like virtual products, AI mini tools, and one-person companies. For example, some articles mentioned people using AI to build Xiaohongshu virtual products around courseware, PPT templates, and niche tool websites. Others are using AI programming to make extremely simple utility websites, charging 9.9 yuan per order and using image-text notes to capture long-tail search traffic.

What moved me most was not the numbers, but the logic behind them: **a product does not have to be huge. As long as it saves a little trouble for a certain type of person in a specific situation, it already has a chance.** Many people start product thinking by wanting to build something big: a platform, a system, a super app. But in the real world, users' needs are often not that grand. Maybe they just want to make a sentence sound better, turn an image into a fuse bead pattern, make a PPT look less ugly, record why they stayed up late again today, or have something organize the noisy thoughts in their head into the next action when they feel anxious. These needs do not look big, but they have one thing in common: **they are very specific.** Specific to a person, a scenario, and an action. And what AI is best at amplifying is exactly this kind of specificity.

I divide these opportunities into three directions.

## The first: turn experience into a button

In the past, how did a person usually deliver their experience? By writing articles, recording courses, doing consulting, or sending resource packs. But all these delivery methods have one problem: the user still has to digest them. You write a great communication framework, and the user may say it is well written, then still fight the same way. You write an analysis of viral Xiaohongshu posts, and the user thinks it makes sense, then opens a blank document and still does not know how to write the first sentence. You explain resume optimization principles for a long time, and the user understands them, but what they actually need is: please directly rewrite this experience for me.

So the essence of skills, AI tools, and automated workflows is not to create more knowledge. It is to turn experience from something explained to you into something that produces a result for you. This is the first direction I mean: **turn experience into a button.** Users do not want to study. Users want to click once and get a result immediately.

For example, turn relationship communication experience into a reply generator, workplace communication experience into a report-polishing tool, writing experience into a title generator, and aesthetic judgment into an image diagnosis tool. This type of product is especially suitable for creators, because what is truly valuable about many creators is not how many principles they have talked about, but how they judge problems over a long period of time. In the past, this way of judging could only be slowly transmitted through articles. Now it can be compressed into a button. You drop in a chat history, and it tells you how to reply next. You paste in a Moments caption, and it makes it less awkward. You upload an image, and it tells you what feels off and how to adjust it.

That is the experience shift. In the past, users bought "I will go back and learn." Now users buy "please directly help me do it." This difference is huge, because people are not that naturally fond of learning. People love having problems solved.

## The second: turn small pain points into toy-like tools

The second category is more interesting. They are not necessarily must-haves, but they create a strong feeling: **I want to try this too.** For example, a desktop digital cat. Does it solve a serious problem? Not really, but it provides a bit of companionship. Or digital worship. It sounds like a joke, but when people are under pressure, they really do need a little ritual. Or a life progress bar: enter your birthday and see how many days you have lived and how many days are left this year. The tool does not complete any work for you, but it turns abstract time into something visible.

The value of this kind of product is not in how complex the function is, but in turning a vague feeling into a small interactive experience. I previously organized some small needs that could be developed into web products, such as a spiritual offering table for today: choose whether you want smooth progress, wealth, or less overthinking today, click to offer incense, and generate a lucky note; a late-night confession room: open it at 2 a.m., type why you are still awake, and it generates an apology letter to tomorrow's self; an emotional weather forecast: enter your state today, and it tells you whether your day is local heavy rain, low pressure, or cloudy turning sunny; an overthinking translator: paste in the messy words in your head, and it translates them into the real problem and the smallest next step.

These things are all small, and some even sound funny. But this category has a strong distribution advantage: **it is screenshot-friendly.** After using it, users may not say, "this tool improved my efficiency," but they may say, "hahaha this is so accurate," "help, this is literally me," or "I want to try this too." This is an important entry point for small products. Do not rush to prove how much commercial value it has. First make people willing to click, play, screenshot, and send it to friends. Many small things do not survive at first because they are urgent needs. They survive because people think, "I want one too."

## The third: make ordinary experiences interactive

There is another type of opportunity. It is not about building a new tool, but about turning an ordinary experience into something participatory. Take novels as an example. In the past, you could only read them. Whatever the author wrote was what you read. But if a novel becomes interactive, you are no longer just a reader. You can choose the plot, influence the characters' fate, and let the story continue according to your choices. Once the experience changes, the user relationship changes too. The user is no longer only consuming content. The user is participating in it.

The same is true for learning materials. In the past, they were PDFs. Users downloaded them and read by themselves. But if they become a small web page where users can ask questions, take quizzes, and generate practice exercises, they are no longer just materials. They become a learning companion. Resume optimization is another example. In the past, it was a template. Now it can become an interactive flow: you enter your experience, it asks what you did, who was affected, and what the result was, then helps you revise it step by step into a version that can be sent out.

The core of this opportunity is: **turn something that can only be viewed into something that can be operated.** Materials can become interactive, stories can become interactive, articles can become interactive, tutorials can become interactive, and even a life experience can become a web page that can be filled in, generated, and revisited. This is especially important for content creators, because much content used to stop at reading, while more and more content in the future will become tools. An article can become a checklist, an opinion can become a tester, a methodology can become a workflow, and a story can become an interactive experience. Content is no longer only read. Content starts being used. This is the part I find most worth watching.

## Why are small needs becoming valuable now?

Because AI has lowered the cost of trial and error. In the past, if you wanted to build a small tool, you first had to ask: is it worth developing specifically? Many answers were no, because development was too expensive. But now it is different. If one person can build an MVP in a few days, the question changes. The question is no longer whether the idea is big enough. It becomes: is it specific enough? Will someone be willing to click it? Is there a real scenario? Can users get a result immediately? Can it be screenshotted, shared, and reused? You will find that the evaluation criteria have completely changed.

In the past, we too easily used the standards of big opportunities to reject small needs. But many real products did not start as big opportunities at all. They started as a small annoyance, a very specific scenario, a moment when someone says, "isn't this exactly me?" This is also what I care about more and more when looking at web product opportunities: do not begin by trying to build an impressive product. First find a specific enough problem. Who is it for? In what scenario? How much are they willing to pay? What problem does it solve? If these four questions cannot be answered, even the flashiest idea is still just an idea. But if they can be answered, even if the idea looks small, it is worth trying.

## The real threshold is not whether you can build, but whether you can see

AI programming will allow more and more people to build things, but being able to build does not mean building the right thing. This is why I do not really believe that casually wrapping something in an AI shell can make money. If you cannot see specific needs, AI will only help you build unwanted things faster. If you do not understand why users click, why they pay, and why they share, AI cannot save you.

The real threshold will move from technology to three places. First, do you understand the scenario? Do you know when users feel pain, where the pain is, and why existing solutions are not good enough? Second, do you have iteration speed? Can you first build a minimum version, send it out, listen to feedback, and then change the next version? Third, do you have a distribution channel? After you build it, who will see it? Who will try it? Who will tell you, "I really want this"? These three things are the moat of small products in the AI era. Not the code itself. Code will become cheaper and cheaper, but your understanding of people will not automatically become cheaper.

## I am now more willing to take weird ideas seriously

In the past, when I saw some weird thoughts, I would filter them out instinctively. For example, a digital offering table, an overthinking translator, a life progress bar, or a small web page that can turn a novel into an interactive plot. Now I will not reject them so quickly, because I have found that many strange-looking ideas are essentially ideas that have not yet been translated into product language.

Behind the digital offering table is the need for ritual in an age of pressure. Behind the overthinking translator is a person's desire to grab one concrete action from chaos. Behind interactive novels is the fact that users are no longer satisfied with passively consuming content. Behind a life autobiography generator is people's desire to understand their own life narrative again. They are not useless. They are just too small, too light, and too ordinary, so in the past they were not worth serious development.

But after AI, the situation has changed. The development cost of small needs has gone down, the validation speed of weird ideas has become faster, and one person's ability to build an idea has become stronger. At this point, opportunity is not necessarily hidden in a grand business plan. It may be hidden in something you casually say one day: "it would be nice if there were something that could help me..." Write that sentence down. Do not rush to laugh at it, and do not rush to package it as a great project. First turn it into a clickable web page, send it out, let 20 people try it, and see whether someone says: "I want this too." From that moment on, it is no longer just a weird idea. It begins to grow into a product.
